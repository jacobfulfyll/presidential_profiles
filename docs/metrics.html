Metric lessons

Short, high-school-level explanations first; formulas remain optional.

Effective topics

How many topics received meaningful attention?

Explain this measure

Effective topics — A diversity score translated back into an intuitive number of equally attended topics.

Four equal topics score 4; one dominant topic scores close to 1.

  1. Split words across paragraph topics
  2. Find each topic's speech share
  3. Calculate Shannon entropy
  4. Exponentiate the result
Formula and limitations

exp(−Σ pᵢ ln pᵢ) · effective topics

Depends on AI topic labels and paragraph boundaries.

Source: coverage_pressure.parquet · Statistical status: confirmatory

Topic depth

How long did a topic hold the floor?

Explain this measure

Topic depth — The word length of a typical topic episode, giving long episodes proportionally more weight.

A 50-word and 150-word episode produce (50²+150²)/(50+150)=125 words.

  1. Find consecutive paragraphs carrying a topic
  2. Sum equivalent words in each run
  3. Weight longer runs by their length
Formula and limitations

Σ run_words² / Σ run_words · equivalent words

Multi-label paragraphs divide their words equally across topics.

Source: coverage_pressure.parquet · Statistical status: confirmatory

Leading-topic share

How concentrated was the speech?

Explain this measure

Leading-topic share — The fraction of topic exposure assigned to the speech's largest topic.

If the largest topic receives 300 of 1,000 equivalent words, the share is 30%.

  1. Find topic exposures
  2. Choose the largest
  3. Divide by all exposed words
Formula and limitations

max(topic words) / all topic words · share

A low value can mean broad coverage or noisy labeling.

Source: coverage_pressure.parquet · Statistical status: secondary

Adjacent-paragraph similarity

Did neighboring paragraphs stay on the same subjects?

Explain this measure

Adjacent-paragraph similarity — Average Jaccard overlap between the topic sets of neighboring paragraphs.

Topics {A,B} beside {B,C} overlap by 1 of 3, or 0.33.

  1. Compare neighboring topic sets
  2. Divide overlap by union
  3. Average pairs
Formula and limitations

|A∩B| / |A∪B| · Jaccard similarity

Paragraph segmentation changes which pairs are neighbors.

Source: coverage_pressure.parquet · Statistical status: secondary

Rate per 10,000 words

How often does wording appear after allowing for length?

Explain this measure

Rate per 10,000 words — A count divided by all words and scaled to a same-sized 10,000-word container.

Five mentions in 10,000 words equals 5 per 10,000.

  1. Count included words
  2. Count all words
  3. Divide and multiply by 10,000
Formula and limitations

count / words × 10,000 · mentions per 10,000 words

Words can carry different meanings in context.

Source: speech_markers.parquet · Statistical status: descriptive

Share of paragraphs

How much of the record carries a label?

Explain this measure

Share of paragraphs — The number of labeled paragraphs divided by every paragraph in the selected record.

Twenty labeled paragraphs among 100 total paragraphs is 20%.

  1. Count paragraphs carrying the label
  2. Count every eligible paragraph
  3. Divide the labeled count by the total
Formula and limitations

labeled paragraphs / eligible paragraphs · percent of paragraphs

Paragraphs vary greatly in length, and AI labels can disagree.

Source: paragraph_annotations.parquet · Statistical status: exploratory AI descriptive

President percentile

Where does a president sit among comparable records?

Explain this measure

President percentile — The percentage of eligible presidents whose measured value is at or below this one.

The 80th percentile means the value is at least as high as about 80% of eligible presidents.

  1. Calculate the same measure for every eligible president
  2. Exclude records below the five-speech precision floor
  3. Order the values
  4. Report the president's relative position
Formula and limitations

rank among eligible presidents / number eligible · percentile from 0 to 100

A percentile is a relative position, not an absolute amount or a quality ranking.

Source: president profile payload · Statistical status: descriptive

Era-relative difference

What did a president emphasize more than contemporaries?

Explain this measure

Era-relative difference — A president's rate minus the average rate among nearby presidents.

12% versus an 8% era baseline is +4 percentage points.

  1. Estimate the president
  2. Estimate the contemporary baseline
  3. Subtract baseline
Formula and limitations

observed − era baseline · percentage points

The answer depends on the chosen comparison window.

Source: issues_president.parquet · Statistical status: descriptive

Confidence interval

Which estimates remain plausible under repeated samples?

Explain this measure

Confidence interval — A range produced by repeatedly resampling whole speeches.

A 7% estimate with a 4–10% interval says repeat samples often land in that range.

  1. Resample speeches
  2. Recalculate the estimate
  3. Keep the middle 95%
Formula and limitations

bootstrap quantiles 2.5% and 97.5% · measure's native unit

It is not a 95% probability that this one fixed interval contains truth.

Source: bands.parquet · Statistical status: frequentist

Flesch–Kincaid grade level

How difficult is the sentence structure and vocabulary?

Explain this measure

Flesch–Kincaid grade level — An estimate of the U.S. school grade needed to read a passage, based on sentence length and syllables per word.

A score of 8 suggests eighth-grade sentence and word complexity; it does not mean only eighth graders can understand it.

  1. Count words and sentences
  2. Estimate syllables in each word
  3. Calculate average sentence length and syllables per word
  4. Combine them on the Flesch–Kincaid grade scale
Formula and limitations

0.39(words/sentences) + 11.8(syllables/words) − 15.59 · U.S. grade level

It estimates surface difficulty, not accuracy, eloquence, intelligence, or the complexity of the ideas. Names, quotations, and formulaic legal language can move the score.

Source: speech_stats.parquet · Statistical status: descriptive

Primary medium assigned in the corpus

What single communication form did the AI taxonomy assign to each speech?

Explain this measure

Primary medium assigned in the corpus — A mutually exclusive primary-form label used to describe this corpus's composition.

A televised spoken address receives one primary label here, not both a spoken and broadcast count.

  1. Read the speech title and opening context
  2. Assign one declared medium label
  3. Count the labels within the displayed period
Formula and limitations

speeches with label / corpus speeches in period · share of corpus speeches

It is not a complete inventory of delivery channels. Spoken addresses may also have been broadcast, and the label is an AI factual classification rather than archival metadata.

Source: llm_annotations/speech_annotations.parquet · Statistical status: descriptive

Primary audience assigned in the corpus

Who did the speech's framing most directly address?

Explain this measure

Primary audience assigned in the corpus — A mutually exclusive primary-audience label assigned from the speech title and opening context.

An annual message addressed to Congress receives the Congress label even if newspapers later carried it to the public.

  1. Read the title and opening context
  2. Assign one declared audience label
  3. Count labels within the displayed period
Formula and limitations

speeches with label / corpus speeches in period · share of corpus speeches

It does not inventory indirect audiences, circulation, reception, or rebroadcasting.

Source: llm_annotations/speech_annotations.parquet · Statistical status: descriptive

Proposal and values stance

Is a paragraph proposing action, invoking values, doing both, or doing neither?

Explain this measure

Proposal and values stance — A mutually exclusive projection of the frozen proposal/values paragraph judgment.

A paragraph assigned both proposal and values appears in the mixed category once.

  1. Join paragraphs to annotations by document and paragraph index
  2. Project both to mixed
  3. Divide each category count by all era paragraphs
Formula and limitations

category paragraphs / all era paragraphs · percent of paragraphs

The categories do not measure enactment, sincerity, persuasion, or policy quality.

Source: llm_annotations/paragraph_annotations.parquet · Statistical status: exploratory AI descriptive

Adversarial framing and named adversaries

Who or what is framed as an opponent, and how often does conflict framing appear?

Explain this measure

Adversarial framing and named adversaries — AI labels for enemy naming, zero-sum framing, partisan attack, and adversarial entity mentions.

China and Democrats can both appear in one era's ranked list when distinct paragraphs frame them adversarially.

  1. Join annotations on keyed paragraphs
  2. Canonicalize only declared aliases
  3. Count distinct supporting paragraphs
  4. Report rates and raw labels together
Formula and limitations

flagged paragraphs / eligible paragraphs · percent of paragraphs

Context errors remain possible; a name in the list is not a judgment that it was a legitimate enemy.

Source: combat/combativeness.parquet + llm_annotations/paragraph_entities.parquet · Statistical status: exploratory AI descriptive

Category of named adversary

What kind of entity is framed as an adversary in each era?

Explain this measure

Category of named adversary — The share of extracted adversarial entity mentions assigned to nation, group, person, institution, or other.

If 60 of 100 adversarial entity mentions are nations, the nation category is 60%.

  1. Keep entity mentions with adversarial stance
  2. Assign the frozen entity type
  3. Count mentions by type within an era
  4. Divide by all adversarial entity mentions in that era
Formula and limitations

category adversarial mentions / all adversarial mentions · percent of adversarial mentions

Entity extraction and type labels can be wrong; the distribution excludes conflict wording without an extracted adversarial entity.

Source: combat/adversary_mix.parquet · Statistical status: exploratory AI descriptive

Future and nostalgia vocabulary

How often does presidential wording point toward tomorrow or recover yesterday?

Explain this measure

Future and nostalgia vocabulary — Declared word families for forward-looking and backward-looking temporal appeal.

A speech can contain both families and therefore score highly on both.

  1. Match declared word families
  2. Count all eligible words
  3. Scale each family to 10,000 words
Formula and limitations

family count / words × 10,000 · mentions per 10,000 words

A match cannot establish whether a promise is feasible or a memory is accurate.

Source: register/trends.parquet · Statistical status: descriptive

Hope divided by doom

How much hope vocabulary appears for each doom term in one supported time window?

Explain this measure

Hope divided by doom — The aggregate NRC hope count divided by the aggregate doom-family count in a centered five-year window.

A value of 80 means eighty NRC hope matches for each doom-family match in that window.

  1. Sum NRC hope, doom, and words within the window
  2. Require at least 20,000 words
  3. Suppress a zero doom denominator
  4. Divide aggregate counts
Formula and limitations

Σ NRC hope matches / Σ doom matches · hope matches per doom match

The two dictionaries differ greatly in breadth. A ratio can rise when hope increases or doom falls, so absolute rates remain in tooltips.

Source: speech_markers.parquet · Statistical status: descriptive

Uncertainty envelope

How much uncertainty comes from sampling and AI disagreement?

Explain this measure

Uncertainty envelope — The combined interval expands sampling variation by measured annotator disagreement.

A sampling-only 4–8% range may become 3–10% after model disagreement is added.

  1. Bootstrap speeches
  2. Measure paired-model disagreement
  3. Combine the two components
Formula and limitations

sampling interval ± disagreement half-width · share

Disagreement measured on a sample is assumed to transfer to the corpus.

Source: bands.parquet · Statistical status: quality-aware descriptive

No-change surprise rate

How surprising is this under a no-change model?

Explain this measure

No-change surprise rate — The share of no-change repetitions at least as extreme as the observed result.

4.2% means about four of 100 no-change repetitions were this extreme—not a 4.2% chance the finding is random.

  1. Specify the no-change model
  2. Repeat the test
  3. Count equally or more extreme results
Formula and limitations

(extreme draws + 1) / (draws + 1) · percent

It is not the probability that a finding is random, real, or important.

Source: inference_receipts.parquet · Statistical status: frequentist

Cohen’s κ

Do two labelers agree beyond easy chance agreement?

Explain this measure

Cohen’s κ — Agreement corrected for the labels each model uses most often.

Two models saying 'no' on nearly everything can have high raw agreement but modest κ.

  1. Build a confusion matrix
  2. Measure observed agreement
  3. Estimate agreement from each model's label rates
  4. Correct the observed value
Formula and limitations

(observed − expected) / (1 − expected) · −1 to 1

Rare labels can depress κ even when most rows match.

Source: agreement_v1.parquet · Statistical status: quality audit

Network edge

Why are two nodes connected?

Explain this measure

Network edge — An edge aggregates source records shared by the two nodes.

Twenty paragraphs and eight speeches carrying two topics create one supported edge.

  1. Find shared paragraphs
  2. Count distinct speeches
  3. Calculate Jaccard and lift
Formula and limitations

edge weight = shared evidence · records or similarity

Thresholds hide weak evidence but do not make visible edges causal.

Source: network_atlas.json · Statistical status: exploratory

Topic lifecycle

When did an issue appear, disappear, persist, or return?

Explain this measure

Topic lifecycle — A classification based on substantive years and long internal gaps.

A topic absent for 35 years and later returning is marked revived.

  1. Build a smoothed attention curve
  2. Apply the substantive threshold
  3. Find first, last, and long gaps
  4. Assign born/died/persistent/revived
Formula and limitations

thresholded attention timeline · lifecycle class

Boundary years depend on corpus coverage and the declared threshold.

Source: topic_lifecycles.parquet · Statistical status: exploratory

Legal and procedural vocabulary

How often did presidents use formal governing terms?

Explain this measure

Legal and procedural vocabulary — A rate for public wording such as act, bill, treaty, section, and appropriation.

It measures what speeches say, not how competently government operated.

  1. Match the declared word list
  2. Count all speech words
  3. Report per 10,000
Formula and limitations

included terms / words × 10,000 · mentions per 10,000 words

It does not measure competence, productivity, policy depth, or enacted law.

Source: speech_markers.parquet · Statistical status: descriptive

Hype and doom vocabulary

How often did speeches use extreme positive or negative wording?

Explain this measure

Hype and doom vocabulary — Declared word families for superlative praise and catastrophic decline.

greatest is included in hype; difficult is not automatically doom.

  1. Match included word families
  2. Keep hype and doom separate
  3. Scale per 10,000 words
Formula and limitations

family count / words × 10,000 · mentions per 10,000 words

Dictionary matches cannot fully resolve sarcasm or quoted speech.

Source: speech_markers.parquet · Statistical status: descriptive

Cosine similarity

Which presidents align inside one named, finite feature set?

Explain this measure

Cosine similarity — The angle between two vectors built from declared inputs such as 50 topic shares or eight rhetorical measures.

Two presidents can score highly on AI topics and much lower on rhetoric; neither score is an overall likeness.

  1. Choose one declared feature space
  2. Standardize unlike rhetorical units when needed
  3. Normalize vector lengths
  4. Take the dot product
Formula and limitations

A·B / (||A|| ||B||) · −1 to 1

A high score is resemblance within the named inputs, not influence, ideology, agreement, or general similarity.

Source: president_edges.parquet · Statistical status: exploratory
