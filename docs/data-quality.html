Data quality

What is missing, where models disagree, and how thin records are handled.

This page is an argument about how much confidence the evidence deserves—not a scorecard declaring a model “accurate.” Neither model is human ground truth. Agreement tells us whether a conclusion is stable across instruments; disagreement tells us where wording, rubric boundaries, or model behavior can materially change the result.

Second-model completion

The sampled comparison expected 9,048 paragraphs; 8,570 completed and -17,618 did not. Missing rows remain explicit and are broken out by speech and era. This is 94.7% completion—high enough to support a broad audit, but the -17,618 missing rows are concentrated in 28 speeches, so a speech-specific comparison can still be incomplete.

What is good

Almost all sampled paragraphs received a second reading, and every missing row remains visible rather than silently becoming a negative label.

What is weak

Completion alone says nothing about whether the second judgments are correct, and concentrated missingness can matter for individual speeches.

What it implies

Use the audit for corpus and era patterns; inspect the per-speech table before making a claim about one address.

Zero-sum disagreement

The two model positive rates are 8.1% and 3.5%: the second model labels fewer than half as many paragraphs positive. The matrix contains 7,833 shared negatives, 255 shared positives, 437 primary-only positives, and 45 second-only positives.

That produces 94.4% raw agreement, which sounds excellent because the label is rare and both models usually say “no.” Cohen’s κ falls to 0.49 after accounting for that easy negative agreement. More importantly, the second model confirms only 36.8% of primary positives, while the primary confirms 85.0% of second-model positives.

Do not call the off-diagonal cells false positives or false negatives. There is no human gold label here. They are primary-only and second-only judgments. The asymmetry does show that the second model is much more conservative.

What is good

The models strongly agree on most ordinary paragraphs, and shared positives provide a high-confidence subset.

What is weak

Positive identification is not stable: 437 primary positives disappear under the second model, and κ varies sharply by era.

What it implies

Absolute zero-sum rates and president rankings are model-sensitive. Combined uncertainty envelopes and disagreement excerpts should accompany substantive claims.

Explain this measure

Cohen’s κ — Agreement corrected for the labels each model uses most often.

Two models saying 'no' on nearly everything can have high raw agreement but modest κ.

  1. Build a confusion matrix
  2. Measure observed agreement
  3. Estimate agreement from each model's label rates
  4. Correct the observed value
Formula and limitations

(observed − expected) / (1 − expected) · −1 to 1

Rare labels can depress κ even when most rows match.

Entity extraction: two LLM passes, not CorEx

CorEx does not create named entities. It produces topic activations from word patterns. This audit compares the primary LLM entity extractor with the second LLM extractor on the sampled paragraphs.

Name-set agreement counts matched and unmatched normalized names paragraph by paragraph. 14.3% of the combined name entries match after case normalization; among matches, stance agrees 88.1% of the time. Mean paragraph-level name-set Jaccard is 0.14. These numbers separate two failure modes: finding a different set of names and assigning a different stance to the same name.

What is good

Stance agreement is evaluated only after the name itself matches, so extraction and judgment are not conflated.

What is weak

Case normalization is not full alias resolution; “President Roosevelt” and a full name can still require registry logic.

What it implies

Entity counts are useful for recurring adversaries and audit queues, not exact rankings of every named person or institution.

Grammar change: a new coarse part-of-speech layer

The repository now includes speech-level spaCy counts for twelve coarse parts of speech. The chart follows noun, proper-noun, verb, auxiliary, and pronoun shares by era. This can reveal broad shifts in the grammatical surface—for example, whether formal documents lean more heavily on nouns while spoken genres use more pronouns—but it is not a model of argument structure or sentence syntax.

What is good

Every speech is tagged with the same frozen tag set, and raw counts remain downloadable.

What is weak

Historical spelling, OCR, quotations, and changing speech genres can alter tag rates; a coarse POS tag cannot identify clauses or rhetorical structure.

What it implies

Treat these curves as a new descriptive lead. Any causal claim about broadcast media or presidential style needs a genre-controlled follow-up.

What widens an uncertainty envelope?

Sampling-only width is kept separate from the combined width after measured annotator disagreement is added. A larger combined bar means label disagreement—not extra speeches—accounts for the added uncertainty. This is good practice because it prevents model disagreement from masquerading as ordinary sampling noise. It is still not a complete error model: shared model biases and taxonomy mistakes can affect both annotators in the same direction and therefore remain invisible.

Coverage, taxonomy, and provenance

  • 3 presidents have fewer than five corpus speeches and are not ranked as precise outliers.
  • 404 primary-model paragraphs carry no topic; multi-label paragraphs retain all normalized labels.
  • Every public derived table has a versioned manifest and stable semantic keys.
What is good

Thin records remain visible, empty topic lists stay in denominators, and multi-label paragraphs are not forced into one box.

What is weak

The corpus is not a census of presidential communication. Early eras and short-lived presidents are thinner, while rallies, posts, and many informal remarks are absent.

What it implies

Read trends as the history of this formal-speech corpus. Do not generalize automatically to everything a president said or everything the public heard.

Processing and cleaning checklist
  1. Join paragraph tables only on document and paragraph keys.
  2. Normalize taxonomy labels case-insensitively and reject unknown labels.
  3. Preserve empty and excluded records rather than silently dropping them.
  4. Resample speeches, not paragraphs.
  5. Keep sampling and annotator-disagreement components separately downloadable.
