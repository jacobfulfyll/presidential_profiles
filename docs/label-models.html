How the label models differ

A detailed, evidence-backed comparison of the anchored CorEx issue model and the LLM semantic taxonomy.

One corpus, two different measuring instruments

Both systems label the same paragraph-sized units and both allow more than one label. They differ in what they can “see.” CorEx is a deterministic, anchored word-pattern model. The LLM layer applies a frozen semantic rubric that can recognize an idea even when its historic wording changes. Neither is ground truth.

CorEx: anchored word patterns

15 hand-defined issues + 7 free topics. Sixteen are public after the free-topic quality gate.

  1. Turn 36,229 paragraphs into binary word-presence vectors.
  2. Remove common English and speech-register words; retain words appearing in at least 20 paragraphs and at most 30% of paragraphs, capped at 25,000 features.
  3. Give each of 15 issues an era-spanning anchor list, with anchor strength 6.
  4. Fit 22 latent factors with seed 42: 15 anchored factors and 7 unsupplied factors.
  5. Store a Boolean activation for every paragraph-topic pair.

Strength: repeatable, cheap, inspectable vocabulary. Blind spot: renamed concepts and meanings that use none of the anchor vocabulary.

LLM: frozen semantic rubric

50 fine topics inside 17 broad domains. Labels were derived from era-isolated corpus samples, frozen, then applied paragraph by paragraph with president, party, and title hidden.

  1. Use embeddings and era-balanced samples to expose subjects the legacy list missed.
  2. Normalize proposals into a closed 50-topic taxonomy with definitions and boundaries.
  3. Give the model only paragraph text and decade, then allow multi-label assignments.
  4. Re-label a stratified speech sample with a second model to measure disagreement.
  5. Carry sampling and annotator disagreement separately, then combine them into the default uncertainty envelope.

Strength: meaning can survive vocabulary drift. Blind spot: subjective rubric judgments, model disagreement, and a more granular taxonomy.

Does CorEx create named entities?

No. CorEx produces paragraph-topic activations from correlated word patterns. It does not emit people, countries, organizations, stance, partisan attack, zero-sum framing, or proposal-versus-values judgments.

Named entities come from a separate LLM extraction pass. The entity agreement numbers compare that primary LLM pass with a second LLM pass on the same sampled paragraphs—they are not CorEx-versus-LLM agreement. The audit separates whether the models found the same normalized names from whether they assigned the same stance after a name matched. See the contextualized entity audit →

Where they agree—and why a mismatch is not automatically an error

The 50 fine LLM topics are projected through a declared many-to-many crosswalk onto the 15 legacy issues. The charts below use all 35,825 comparable paragraphs. A paragraph can activate several issues in either system.

Explain this measure

Cohen’s κ — Agreement corrected for the labels each model uses most often.

Two models saying 'no' on nearly everything can have high raw agreement but modest κ.

  1. Build a confusion matrix
  2. Measure observed agreement
  3. Estimate agreement from each model's label rates
  4. Correct the observed value
Formula and limitations

(observed − expected) / (1 − expected) · −1 to 1

Rare labels can depress κ even when most rows match.

Inspect the evidence

Download paragraph-level agreement summaries · Download speech-level method compositions. Jaccard is overlap divided by the union; κ adjusts observed agreement for the two systems' label rates.

The preregistered coherence prediction failed. Anchor-word coherence did not predict cross-method agreement (Spearman ρ = −0.054 for Jaccard, no-change surprise 85.0%). Immigration is the clearest counterexample: its anchor words rarely co-occur, yet its CorEx and LLM labels agree relatively well. That is evidence that word co-occurrence and label precision are different questions.

Which view should you use?

  • Use CorEx for the long-run broad issue biographies and a completely deterministic historical lens.
  • Use the LLM taxonomy for finer subjects, semantic drift, proposals/values, and paragraph judgments—while keeping its uncertainty envelopes visible.
  • Use the comparison when a claim depends on one instrument. Agreement supports robustness; disagreement identifies a place to inspect wording, taxonomy boundaries, and source paragraphs.
